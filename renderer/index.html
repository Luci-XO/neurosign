Quick facts

Parkinson's disease develops when nerve cells that produce dopamine, a chemical messenger involved in controlling movement, gradually stop working properly in a part of the brain called the substantia nigra.

Common early signs include a slight tremor in one hand, stiffness or slowness of movement, smaller handwriting, a softer or more monotone voice, and reduced facial expression. Symptoms usually appear gradually and are easy to miss at first.

There is currently no cure, but medication, physical therapy, and lifestyle changes can manage symptoms effectively, especially when started early. Diagnosis and treatment decisions should always involve a qualified doctor.

The History of Parkinson's Disease

The condition was first described in 1817 by James Parkinson, a London surgeon, in a short essay he titled "An Essay on the Shaking Palsy." He documented a handful of patients with a characteristic trembling and stooped gait, without yet understanding what caused it.

Later in the 19th century, the French neurologist Jean-Martin Charcot studied the condition extensively between 1868 and 1881 and is credited with giving it the name "Parkinson's disease," helping distinguish it from other movement disorders of the time.

The breakthrough in understanding its biology came in the late 1950s and 1960, when researchers including Arvid Carlsson, Oleh Hornykiewicz, and Herbert Ehringer found that the brains of people with Parkinson's had markedly reduced levels of dopamine. This finding led directly to the development of Levodopa therapy through the 1960s, a treatment that remains a cornerstone of Parkinson's care today.

Research since then has continued to refine diagnosis, deepen understanding of non-movement symptoms, and explore new treatments - work that is still ongoing today.

This summary is written for general awareness and is not medical advice. For the complete, continuously updated article, search "Parkinson's disease" on Wikipedia (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parkinson's_disease).
